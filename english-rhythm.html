Background

Why English rhythm feels unfamiliar

Spanish and Catalan give each syllable about the same length, so a sentence keeps a fairly steady pace. English makes the stressed syllables longer and shortens everything in between. Most of the difficulty students have with English rhythm starts here.

Spanish: nine syllables, nine beats. Spanish has stress too, and NI, MI and PAN are louder than the rest. But a louder syllable does not make its neighbours shorter, and every vowel keeps its full quality.

English: eight syllables, three beats. The five unstressed syllables share the time between the beats, and their vowels reduce to /ə/.

An advantage for Catalan speakers

Eastern Catalan already reduces unstressed a and e to /ə/, which is the vowel you hear in pare, casa and dona. You already produce the schwa, so the thing to learn is where English puts it, not how to make it. Spanish has no reduction of this kind, and Western Catalan, towards Lleida and the Ebre, does not reduce vowels in the same way.

A habit that comes from Spanish

Spanish words do not begin with sp-, st- or sk-, so a vowel often appears in front of them: espeak, estudent, Espanish. This adds a syllable that the English rhythm has no room for.

Why it matters
An extra syllable pushes the beat out of position. The listener then has to re-time the sentence before they can follow it.

Layer 2 of 6 Word stress

Which syllable carries the stress

Any English word with more than one syllable has one syllable that is stronger than the rest. Spanish and Catalan mark stress with a written accent when it is needed. English does not mark it at all, so you have to learn the stress together with the word.

Stress can change the part of speech

In a large group of two-syllable words, the noun is stressed on the first syllable and the verb on the second.

a REcord · to reCORD
a PREsent · to preSENT
an INcrease · to inCREASE
an EXport · to exPORT

Compound nouns are stressed on the first part

a GREENhouse — a building for plants
a green HOUSE — a house painted green

Each pair uses the same two words with different meanings, and the stress is what separates them. The same applies to a BLACKbird and a black BIRD.

This is a tendency, not a rule, but some endings fix the position of the stress. The endings -ion, -ic, -ity and -ical put it on the syllable immediately before them: inforMAtion, ecoNOmic, possiBIlity. Learning the ending saves learning each word separately.

Layer 3 of 6 Content and function words

Which words are stressed in a sentence

In a sentence, English treats words in two different ways. One group of words takes a beat. The other does not.

Content words — stressed

nouns · main verbs · adjectives · adverbs · question words · negatives (not, never, -n't) · demonstratives (this, that)

These words tell the listener what you are talking about. Remove them and the message is lost.

Function words — unstressed

articles (a, the) · prepositions (of, at, for) · auxiliary verbs (have, was, can) · pronouns (he, them) · conjunctions (and, but) · to

These words hold the sentence together. A listener can usually predict them, so English spends very little time on them.

3
The stress falls on the words that carry the meaning. A word that answers who, what, which, how or when is almost always stressed. A word whose job is to connect other words is almost always unstressed.
There is one exception, covered in Layer 6. Any word can be stressed if you are contrasting it with something else: I said it was on the table, not under it. A contrast always takes priority over the pattern above.

Layer 5 of 6 Connected speech

How sounds change between words

The unstressed parts of a sentence are said quickly, so sounds at the edges of words run together, drop out or change. This is not careless speech. It is how the language normally sounds at conversational speed.

Linking

A consonant at the end of one word joins onto a vowel at the start of the next. The gap between the two words disappears.

an‿apple   pick‿it‿up   far‿away

/əˈnæpl/ · /pɪkɪˈtʌp/ · /fɑːrəˈweɪ/

In far away a British speaker pronounces the r that would otherwise be silent, because a vowel follows it.

Elision — a sound disappears

/t/ and /d/ are the sounds most often lost, usually when they are caught between two other consonants.

next day
sandwich
must be

/ˈneks deɪ/ · /ˈsænwɪdʒ/ · /məs ˈbi/

Assimilation — a sound changes

A sound changes so that it is easier to say next to the one that follows it.

ten boys → /tem bɔɪz/
good girl → /ɡʊɡ ɡɜːl/
would you → /ˈwʊdʒə/

The last pattern also gives did you and last year. Students miss this one most often when they are listening.

5
This matters more for listening than for speaking. You will be understood without it. But if you are listening for four separate words in What did you do?, you will miss /ˈwɒdʒədʊ/. Students usually describe this as native speakers talking too fast.
Layer 6 of 6 Tone units

Chunks and the main stress

We speak in short chunks rather than in whole sentences. Each chunk has one syllable that stands out more than the others, and that is where the listener's attention goes. If you move it, the meaning changes.

The usual position is the last content word

When I arRIVED | the door was Open.

There are two chunks here, each with its own beats and one syllable stronger than the rest. Nothing is being emphasised. This is how the sentence sounds when no special meaning is attached to it.

I didn't say she stole it.

Someone else said it.

I didn't say SHE stole it.

I said somebody else did.

I didn't say she STOLE it.

She borrowed it, perhaps.

the same six words, with the stress in a different place

6
Where you put the main stress is a choice, and it is usually the last part of the system students get control of. In the usual place it sounds neutral. Anywhere else it marks a contrast, whether or not you meant one. This is why a main stress in the wrong place can sound argumentative.

English counts stresses. The opening line of Shakespeare's Sonnet 18 has ten syllables arranged as five weak–strong pairs: de‑DUM de‑DUM de‑DUM de‑DUM de‑DUM. English alternates strong and weak syllables naturally, so the metre follows the language.

Spanish counts syllables. This line from Jorge Manrique's Coplas (1476) is an octosílabo, a line of eight syllables. Spanish metres are named after a number because each syllable takes about the same time to say. Catalan verse counts syllables as well, although it stops counting at the last stressed one.

The research

How accurate is this model?

English is usually described as stress-timed and Spanish as syllable-timed, as though the beats in English fell at regular intervals like a metronome. When phoneticians measured this, they did not find that regularity.

What the measurements showed

The intervals between stressed syllables in English are not equal when you time them. The strict form of the theory was tested in the 1980s and was not supported. Languages are now usually placed along a continuum instead of in two separate categories.

The real differences

Two differences are real and measurable. English reduces its unstressed vowels heavily and Spanish does not reduce them at all. English also allows much more crowded consonant clusters, so its syllables vary far more in length.

Together these two facts produce the impression of a regular beat.

✓
The model is still worth teaching. If you tell a student to keep the stresses even and compress everything between them, you get the right result. It works as an instruction even though it is a rough description, and it seems better to say that than to claim a precision the research does not support.
Summary

The six layers together

1 · Syllables

Count the vowel sounds you say, not the letters you see. Chocolate has two.

2 · Word stress

One syllable in each word is the strong one. English does not mark it in writing, so it has to be learnt with the word.

3 · Content and function words

The beat falls on the words that carry meaning. The grammar words do not get one.

4 · Weak forms and the schwa

Unstressed words change their vowel to /ə/, unless they come at the end of a clause.

5 · Connected speech

At conversational speed, sounds link, drop and change across word boundaries. This matters mostly for listening.

6 · Tone units

Speech comes in chunks, each with one main stress, and moving that stress changes the meaning.

♪
The most useful change is to stop giving every syllable the same weight. Make the stressed syllables longer than feels comfortable and let the rest shrink. English sounds wrong when it is even.
Three things worth practising this week

1. Take a sentence you have written and mark only the words that carry meaning, then say it with a beat on those words and on nothing else.
2. Collect five weak forms from a song or a podcast and write down what they sound like rather than how they are spelt.
3. Say a long word slowly and then at speed, and notice which vowel disappears first. It is almost always the one next to the stressed syllable.
